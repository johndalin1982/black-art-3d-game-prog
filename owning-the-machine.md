# Owning the Machine

### Microsoft, the games platform, and the strategy from DOS to Windows to DirectX to Xbox

## Abstract

For about fifteen years, from the mid-1980s to 2001, Microsoft worked to own the platform that PC games were built on. This essay treats that effort as a contest between two forces: what Microsoft wanted commercially — to own the platform, lock in developers, and control the programming interface — and what the hardware of each era allowed. Because the two are inseparable, the essay stays close to the machine: how the PC's hardware, drivers, operating systems, and graphics APIs worked, and why that engineering repeatedly forced Microsoft's hand. Its central claim concerns a single shift. Control of the hardware moved from the application to the operating system, pushed there by the change from real-mode DOS to protected-mode Windows, and that move created both the prize Microsoft wanted and the technical problem it had to solve. DirectX (1995) solved that problem by giving games a fast path to the hardware through a driver Microsoft controlled, and so pulled developers off DOS and made Microsoft's API the point at which the platform was won. The later sections follow the consequences: the alternatives Microsoft tried and abandoned (WinG, DCI, OpenGL, Talisman, Fahrenheit); the hardware shake-out that left NVIDIA and ATI standing while 3dfx collapsed; and the two events of 2001 — the Xbox, a defensive move into consoles built on the same DirectX stack, and Windows XP, which retired DOS on the desktop. The console move succeeded: from a standing start Microsoft built a business that has held a top-two place against Sony ever since, a formidable competitor if never the outright leader.

*A note on evidence.* The Windows driver mechanisms come from primary vendor documentation and were checked through multi-vote adversarial review. The 3dfx and Xbox history rests on primary and reputable secondary sources, with dates fixed and two common overstatements — the "first GPU" and Gates's supposed obsession with gaming — corrected in place. The strategic accounts draw partly on interested first-person testimony, flagged as such. The hardware and operating-system details in Sections 2 through 7 are standard, textbook-level material cited to canonical references. The closing note on method gives the full evidentiary basis.

## 1. Platform ownership as the organizing problem

Over the 1990s, control of the PC's hardware — the right to address the screen, the sound card, and the disk directly — passed from the application to the operating system. Under MS-DOS, a game drove the hardware itself. Under the Windows systems that succeeded it, the operating system and its drivers stood in between. This change is usually described as technical progress, but it is better understood as the transfer of a commercial asset: the platform on which games are written and sold. Microsoft pursued that asset deliberately. Its DirectX evangelist later called the program a "Manhattan Project," meant to make the platform "crack for developers" and to "displace Japanese game consoles with PCs and ultimately the Xbox" [1][2]; behind the rhetoric the strategy is plain — capture PC game development with DirectX, then carry the same model into the console with the Xbox.

Two variables run through the analysis. The first is commercial: ownership of the platform locks in developers, confers control of the API and so of the platform, allows competing standards to be displaced, and protects an existing franchise. The second is technical: in any period the hardware can do only so much, and at a cost. The technical variable cannot be judged without a working account of the machine, which Section 2 supplies; the sections after it trace, at each stage, a commercial aim meeting a hardware limit. Three further factors recur: backward compatibility and the weight of the installed base; the economics of standards, in which one driver per device and one API per market prove decisive; and a reliability requirement that steadily moves hardware access behind the operating system.

The PC did not win the home because it was the best machine; for years it was not. The Amiga did graphics and sound the PC could not touch, and anyone who saw one in the late 1980s knew it. What the PC had instead was that no one owned it. A clone industry was free to build it, undercut itself, and flood the market, and that flood is what a developer follows: the better machine sold to enthusiasts, the open one sold to everyone, and the games went where the buyers were [87]. The 256-colour VGA standard of 1987 closed the graphics gap, and after that the rivals fell on business grounds rather than merit — Commodore bankrupt in 1994, Atari fading, Apple keeping the Macintosh closed, dear, and pointed at work rather than play [88][89]. One technical edge did come to matter, but later: the floating-point performance of the x86 line, climbing through the 386, 486, and Pentium, turned decisive only once 3D games arrived and the CPU had to transform every vertex (Section 7). The PC won first on economics and only afterward on power. This was the machine Microsoft set out to own.

The whole argument follows one boundary — control of the hardware — as it climbs the stack over time:

```
 DOS (to ~1995) — the application owns the hardware
   Game ──[ mov 0xA0000, IN/OUT, hooked INTs ]──▶ Hardware (VGA, Sound Blaster, NIC)
   Game ┄┄[ slow, optional ]┄┄▶ BIOS ┄┄▶ Hardware

 Windows 95 + DirectX — the OS owns the hardware
   Game ──▶ DirectX runtime (DirectDraw / Direct3D) ──▶ Vendor HAL (display driver)
        ──[ DMA command buffer ]──▶ 3D accelerator / VRAM

 Xbox (2001) — a fixed DirectX platform
   Game ──▶ Direct3D (Xbox variant) ──▶ NVIDIA NV2A + NT-kernel OS

 The boundary — who controls the hardware — climbs the stack over time:
 from the game itself, to the OS driver, to a fixed OS-owned box.
```

## 2. How a PC works: the stack from silicon to application

The arguments that follow rest on four facts about the PC: how the processor enforces — or fails to enforce — privilege, how memory and devices are addressed, how a device signals the processor and moves data, and how these layers stack up. This section sets them out.

**Processor modes and privilege.** The 8086 and its successors start in *real mode*: a 20-bit segmented address space reaching one megabyte, with no memory protection and no privilege levels, so any instruction can touch any address or I/O port [3]. The 80286 and 80386 added *protected mode*, which brings hardware-enforced privilege rings and, on the 386, paging through a memory-management unit (MMU) [4]. The x86 defines four rings, but mainstream operating systems use only two: ring 0 for the kernel and ring 3 for user code [5]. The point that matters most here is that, in protected mode, a ring-3 program cannot run privileged instructions or reach I/O ports outside its allowed set; any attempt *traps* to ring 0. Real mode has no such trap, and that single absence is what let Windows enforce a driver model and kept DOS from doing so.

**The memory map.** The original PC set aside the top 384 KB of the first megabyte for device memory and firmware, leaving 640 KB of "conventional" RAM for programs [6]. Graphics hardware sits in the reserved region: the VGA framebuffer is mapped at physical address `0xA0000`, so a write to that memory is a write to the screen.

```
 0xFFFFF ┌─────────────────────────────┐
         │  System BIOS ROM             │  F0000–FFFFF
         │  Adapter option ROMs         │  C0000–EFFFF
 0xB8000 │  Colour text framebuffer     │  B8000–BFFFF
 0xA0000 │  VGA graphics framebuffer    │  A0000–AFFFF  ← Mode 13h writes here
         ├─────────────────────────────┤
         │  Conventional RAM (640 KB)   │  00000–9FFFF
 0x00000 └─────────────────────────────┘
```

**Addressing and moving data.** A device is reached in one of three ways. *Port-mapped I/O* uses the dedicated `IN` and `OUT` instructions against a 16-bit I/O address space; this is how control registers are programmed, and the path protected mode can gate [8]. *Memory-mapped I/O* maps device memory into the physical address space, as with the VGA framebuffer. A device signals the processor with an *interrupt*, routed by the 8259 programmable interrupt controller to a vector in the interrupt table; this is how a sound card says its buffer is empty, or a disk that a read is done [9]. Finally, bulk data moves by *direct memory access* (DMA): instead of the CPU copying each byte, a DMA controller — or, on the PCI bus, the device itself acting as *bus master* — moves data to and from memory on its own while the processor does other work [10][11]. The difference between feeding a device one register at a time and letting it pull data by DMA is, as Section 5 shows, the difference between DOS-era and DirectX-era graphics throughput.

**The driver.** A *driver* is the code that knows a device's registers and protocol and presents a uniform interface above them. The questions the rest of the essay turns on are where that driver lives, who controls the interface it presents, and whether the application must go through it or is merely *invited* to. Under DOS it was invited; under the protected operating systems it is *required*. (DOS did ship one real loadable-driver mechanism, the installable `.SYS` driver named in `CONFIG.SYS`, but it covered disk and console devices, not the graphics and audio paths games used [12].)

## 3. DOS: hardware access without an operating-system boundary

Under DOS, the application owned the hardware — and nothing had decided that it should. Real mode cannot trap a program that reaches for a device it has no right to (Section 2), so DOS could not have enforced a boundary even had it wanted one; it loaded programs, managed files, and left the hardware to whoever reached it first. Support for that hardware was improvised, at three levels.

```
 DOS application / game
   ├─ Level 1 (slow, device-independent) ──▶ BIOS  (INT 10h video, 13h disk, 16h keyboard)
   ├─ Level 2 (resident drivers, spare vectors) ──▶ TSRs  (mouse INT 33h, packet drivers, VESA VBE)
   └─ Level 3 (fastest, fully manual) ──▶ direct port I/O (IN/OUT) + framebuffer writes to 0xA0000
        ( all three reach the Hardware )
```

The slow, safe level was the BIOS, which exposed device-independent routines through software interrupts [7]. Above it, programs that stayed resident in memory hung their own interfaces on spare interrupt vectors — the mouse on `INT 33h`, packet drivers for the network, the VESA extensions for video [17][19]. Beneath both, where speed decided everything, code spoke to the chip directly. The price of that freedom was fragmentation. Because nothing in the machine spoke for the sound hardware, every game had to carry its own support for every card, with the card's port, IRQ, and DMA channel fixed into the driver when the user installed the game; set one wrong and the game fell silent, since no part of the system was answerable for the device.

**Why Mode 13h mattered.** Graphics is where this directness paid off most, and the reason lies in the shape of the VGA. The card is not just memory but a small pipeline: the CPU writes pixels, the *CRT controller* (CRTC) scans them out, and a 256-entry palette in the *RAMDAC* turns each stored value into the red-green-blue the monitor shows [13][16].

```
 CPU ──writes──▶ VGA memory ──scanned by──▶ CRTC ──indices──▶ palette/RAMDAC ──RGB──▶ monitor
                 (0xA0000, 64 KB window)        (timing, retrace)      (256 of 262144 colours)
```

Driving that pipeline is usually awkward. VGA memory is split into four parallel *bit-planes*, and in the 16-colour modes a single pixel is spread across all four, reachable only through latch registers and several operations apiece — flexible, and far too slow for a game [14]. Mode 13h removes the obstacle: it chains the planes into one flat array of 320 × 200 bytes, one byte to a pixel, so drawing a pixel becomes a single `mov` to `0xA0000` with no register games at all. That is why nearly every DOS action game ran in Mode 13h. The unchained "Mode X" variant won back square pixels and page-flipping at the cost of returning to planar addressing [15]; and the higher Super VGA resolutions brought the awkwardness back in full, hiding the framebuffer behind a 64 KB window that had to be paged one bank at a time through the VESA BIOS [17]. The next era's accelerators would remove that friction.

**The cost of leaving real mode.** One more constraint shaped the era. Real mode could address only 640 KB, so to use more, commercial games ran under a *DOS extender*, which put the program into 32-bit protected mode and brokered its calls back down to real-mode DOS and the BIOS [18]. Every such call down — every `INT 10h` to the VESA BIOS — cost a switch to real mode and back. Directness was not merely preferred, then; the alternative was measurably slow.

**Why graphics stayed direct while sound did not.** It is tempting to say DOS could not support drivers at all. But it could, and for sound it did. With no system audio interface, middleware grew up to cover dozens of incompatible cards behind one set of calls: John Ratcliff's DIGPAK reached games through a spare interrupt, while John Miles' Audio Interface Library and HMI's Sound Operating System linked into the program and loaded a driver per card at run time [92][93]. The mouse driver and the VESA extensions did the same for input and for setting video modes. Drivers, then, were possible on DOS. The sharper question is why graphics — the one path where speed mattered most — never got one.

Three things kept it direct, and together they explain what came after. First, graphics did not need an abstraction the way sound did: IBM's VGA was a standard every clone copied, so a game could write the framebuffer on any card and still run everywhere [94], while sound had no such anchor — AdLib's FM, the Sound Blaster's DMA, the Gravis Ultrasound's wavetable, and Roland's MIDI were four unrelated designs, and only that kind of chaos forces an abstraction into being. Second, the abstraction graphics might have used was too slow to use: the BIOS could plot any pixel, but a call for each one, in a loop that runs millions of times a frame, cost orders of magnitude more than a direct store [94]. Sound could absorb a little of that overhead, because the card played its buffer on its own — though even there, mixing several voices fell back on the CPU, which is why the Gravis Ultrasound's hardware mixing was prized for the work it lifted off the processor [95]. Graphics could absorb none.

The third reason is the one that matters for everything else. An abstraction earns its cost by handing work to hardware, and in the DOS years the graphics chip did no work — the CPU computed and wrote every pixel, and the card was only a place to put them. With nothing to hand off, any layer over the framebuffer could only add cost, so the most direct design was also the only sensible one, and no driver — and so no platform owner — could take hold on the path a game used to draw. The same logic appears one tier up, where graphics did fragment. Above plain VGA, Super VGA splintered as badly as sound, and an abstraction appeared at once to cover it: the VESA BIOS Extensions, and behind them a small industry — SciTech's UniVBE, Ted Gruber's Fastgraph — that spanned scores of chips behind a single interface [96][97]. But those layers abstracted only the setup: choosing a mode, finding the framebuffer, paging the window. The drawing still fell to the CPU, because there was still no drawing hardware to give it to, and VESA's lone attempt at an acceleration standard, VBE/AF, went almost unimplemented and was abandoned (Section 6). A graphics driver would be worth its overhead only once the chip had something to do — the condition DirectDraw and Direct3D were built to meet (Section 5).

That recasts what DOS speed actually was. Mode 13h was fast, but only because a pixel was a single store; 3D ran entirely on the CPU, bounded by how quickly the processor could fill pixels, with Super VGA's paging tax on top. DOS was not fast in general; it was only unmediated. That is why developers felt no loyalty to it. id Software's John Romero, by his own later account in David Kushner's *Masters of Doom*, summed up the system as "DOS Blows" [91]; what kept games there was not affection but the absence of anything in the way. Whatever platform could offer the same unmediated speed without the drawbacks would take them, and one soon did: id, the standard-bearer of DOS gaming, moved to Windows (Section 5). Microsoft's problem was now stated for it — reproduce direct-to-hardware speed from inside an operating system that, by design, no longer permitted it.

## 4. Windows 3.x and Windows 95: the protected-mode transition and the games problem

Windows, in its early form, held game development back rather than enabling it, for reasons that are now mechanical. Windows 3.x drew through the *Graphics Device Interface* (GDI): an application drew into a device-independent *device context*, and GDI translated those calls for the display through a 16-bit `.DRV` display driver [22]. The abstraction was the whole point — the same code drew to any screen or printer — but its bitmap operations were much slower than writing the framebuffer directly, which is just what a game needs. Below the desktop, 386 Enhanced mode added a protected-mode supervisor and the *virtual device driver* (VxD) model, which ran DOS programs in virtual-8086 mode with privileged instructions trapped to ring 0 — the lineage shown by the change of file extension from `.386` to `.vxd` [20][21]. None of this gave native applications a fast path to the display.

Windows 95 raised both the commercial stake and the technical contradiction. It was a 32-bit consumer operating system Microsoft needed games to fill, with preemptive multitasking and a Plug-and-Play subsystem that took over the assignment of I/O ports, interrupts, and DMA channels users had once set by hand [23][25]. Its driver model kept real compromises for the sake of compatibility: VxDs ran in a single ring-0 address space with no memory protection between drivers, and a real-mode DOS component stayed at boot while 32-bit code handled most requests — what Raymond Chen has called a compatibility decoy [23][24]. These compromises were a commercial necessity, because an operating system that breaks the installed base cannot be sold; backward compatibility constrained the engineering. The contradiction was now plain: memory protection is exactly what blocks the direct hardware access games relied on, and yet Microsoft needed games on Windows. The platform needed a sanctioned, low-latency path to the graphics hardware — a driver contract that delivered DOS-class performance under the operating system's control. DirectX was built to be that contract.

## 5. DirectX: the driver contract and the controlled fast path

DirectX resolved the conflict between protection and performance, and made Microsoft's API the point of control over the platform. The commercial goal was explicit — hardware-level performance on Windows, so that developers would move off DOS and Windows would become the place games were built [1][2] — and the technology, built by a group led by Alex St. John, Craig Eisler, and Eric Engstrom, shipped as the Windows Games SDK on 30 September 1995 [26][27]; by about 2000 it had displaced DOS for game development [28]. Microsoft pushed it as a campaign: at an internal "Judgment Day" event on 30 October 1995, St. John showed a promotional video that dropped Bill Gates into a level of *Doom* — leather coat and shotgun — in which Gates shoots a demon and asks "Who do you want to execute today?", a riff on the "Where do you want to go today?" campaign [29]. The message matched the reality: *Doom* was installed on more PCs than Windows 95, so Microsoft needed games more than games needed Microsoft [30]. Developers followed the technology, not the brand. id Software, whose Doom had defined DOS gaming, ported it to Windows 95 as Doom95 — the first published DirectX game — and a year later shipped GLQuake on hardware-accelerated OpenGL [28][52]. Even id's lead programmer, John Carmack, who would soon attack Direct3D in blunt terms (Section 6), moved the studio onto Windows. The shift showed that a developer base often assumed to be wedded to DOS was in fact pragmatic: it would take whatever interface gave performance with the fewest drawbacks; building the right interface was therefore enough to move the platform.

**The HAL/HEL contract.** The technical mechanism is the display driver's device-driver interface (DDI). DirectDraw and Direct3D turned graphics acceleration into a *Hardware Abstraction Layer* (HAL) and a *Hardware Emulation Layer* (HEL). In Microsoft's words, the HAL "is a device-specific interface usually provided by the display hardware manufacturer," "contains only device-dependent code and performs no emulation," and reports *capability bits*, so that "if a function is not performed by the hardware, the DirectDraw driver does not report it as a hardware capability"; unsupported operations fall through to the HEL for software emulation, and the HAL can live either in the display driver or in a separate DLL [31][32]. This is a *controlled fast path*: near-hardware performance through a vendor driver under the operating system's control, rather than by direct register access. Its commercial weight equals its technical weight, because one driver, written once by the hardware vendor, serves every DirectX application, and the application targets the API, not the device. Control of that interface is control of the platform.

**Why the contract scaled.** The decisive thing about this arrangement is economic, and it is what no DOS standard could match: it spreads the work of writing drivers across the hardware industry and gives each part to the company best placed to do it. Microsoft defines the contract once; each vendor then writes a *single* driver, for its own device — the only hardware it has the documentation for and the reason to optimise — while the capability bits let one API cover devices of differing ability without the application having to tell them apart. N vendors write N drivers, each by the best-informed author, and the game targets the API, not the device. What makes this hold is structural, not contractual: under Windows a graphics card cannot work at all without a vendor driver written to Microsoft's interface, so the whole industry is on the contract by necessity — the later "Designed for Windows" logo and WHQL testing were a secondary nudge toward quality, not the lever. The DOS world had no equivalent, and its closest analogue ran backwards: third parties such as SciTech wrote and maintained driver software for well over a hundred display chipsets they did not make, under per-vendor non-disclosure agreements, forever chasing new hardware (Section 3) — one outsider carrying the whole industry's driver burden, which could not scale and in time wound down. The contract was never *only* a commercial arrangement, however. It also relied on operating-system services DOS lacked — protected-mode memory management, Plug-and-Play resource arbitration [23][25], and, for textures, an OS-managed memory-remapping service for the graphics card (the AGP GART, described later in this section) [42][43] — so the platform was as much systems engineering as commerce. And Microsoft was no passive publisher of an interface: it wrote and maintained the larger, shared part of the stack — the runtime, the software reference rasteriser, and the emulation layer that fills in when hardware lacks a feature [31][32] — and shaped the API together with the hardware vendors rather than dictating it, as the developers' public criticism of early Direct3D (Section 6) made plain.

```
 Windows / DirectX — authorship delegated to each vendor  (SCALES)
   Microsoft defines ONE contract: the DDI + capability bits
     NVIDIA  ──▶ writes 1 driver for its own chip ─┐
     ATI     ──▶ writes 1 driver for its own chip ─┤
     Matrox  ──▶ writes 1 driver for its own chip ─┼──▶ DirectX API ──▶ any game targets the API
     S3      ──▶ writes 1 driver for its own chip ─┘

 DOS — one third party carries every vendor's driver  (DOES NOT SCALE)
     Tseng, Cirrus, Trident, Paradise, ... (every chip)
        └──▶ SciTech writes & maintains N drivers for chips it did not make (per-vendor NDAs)
               └──▶ game: partial coverage, perpetual catch-up
```

**The pipeline and the draw call.** Direct3D defines a rendering *pipeline*: per-vertex transform and lighting, triangle setup and clipping, rasterisation into fragments, texture sampling, and blending into the framebuffer [33][34][35], with a *depth (z) buffer* to decide which surface is visible at each pixel [36]. The application drives this pipeline in *immediate mode*. Early Direct3D submitted work through clumsy *execute buffers*; DirectX 5 (1997) replaced them with DrawPrimitive, which "completely sidesteps execute buffers while providing its own path to the rendering facilities of the hardware" [37]. Below DrawPrimitive, the driver-facing contract is the `D3dDrawPrimitives2` callback, which takes a *command buffer* (`lpDDCommands` in a `D3DHAL_DRAWPRIMITIVES2DATA` structure) made of `D3DHAL_DP2COMMAND` records identified by a `bCommand` field; texture upload is itself a token in the stream, `D3DDP2OP_TEXBLT`, telling the driver to move a texture into video memory [38][39]. Surfaces — primary, back-buffer, and offscreen — live in video memory and are shown by hardware *page-flipping* [40].

```
 1. Game (app)        ── DrawPrimitive(...) / SetTexture(...) ──────────▶ Direct3D runtime
 2. Direct3D runtime  ── D3dDrawPrimitives2(command buffer: DP2 + TEXBLT) ▶ Vendor HAL (display driver)
 3. Vendor HAL        ── bus-master DMA of the command buffer ───────────▶ Accelerator + VRAM
 4. Accelerator       :  transform ─▶ setup ─▶ rasterise ─▶ texture ─▶ z-test ─▶ blend
 5. Accelerator       :  write the back-buffer surface in VRAM
 6. Accelerator       ── page-flip ─▶ scanout (RAMDAC) ──────────────────▶ Monitor
```

**Why this beat the DOS path.** The contrast with the DOS path is the core of the performance argument. A DOS game at Super VGA resolution issued a BIOS `INT 10h` per operation, each paying a real-mode switch, and the CPU did all the 3D math besides. DirectX inverts both: the runtime *batches* thousands of operations into a command buffer that the device consumes on its own by *bus-master DMA* [10][11], and dedicated silicon does the rasterisation. The bus supplied the bandwidth. Plain PCI offered about 132 MB/s of shared bandwidth [41]; the Accelerated Graphics Port (1997), designed for texture traffic, gave the graphics card a dedicated channel and, through the Graphics Address Remapping Table, let it read textures straight from system memory (AGP texturing), scaling toward about 2 GB/s [42][43].

```
 DOS:     CPU --INT 10h (real-mode transition) per call--> BIOS --> VGA      [serial, taxed per call; CPU also rasterises]
 DirectX: CPU --writes one command buffer--> [bus-master DMA] --> GPU pipeline --> VRAM   [batched, asynchronous; GPU rasterises]
```

Dedicated hardware did the rasterisation; the bus, the DMA engine, and the command buffer moved the data; and the DirectX driver coordinated all three — delivering, on Microsoft's own interface, performance the open DOS model could not reach.

## 6. The competing approaches and standards DirectX displaced

DirectX won out over several alternatives — Microsoft's own projects and the industry's main standard alike — each a different point on the trade-off between commercial and technical aims.

**SVGA and the VESA BIOS Extensions: the open standard at the wrong layer.** Before DirectDraw, the way to reach graphics beyond Mode 13h was not a Microsoft product but an industry standard, and its defeat is especially instructive, because it was the kind of vendor-neutral standard a market is supposed to reward. After VGA, the display market split into incompatible "Super VGA" chips — S3, Trident, Cirrus Logic, Tseng, and others — each offering higher resolutions and colour depths, but each with its own register interface and its own scheme for banking a framebuffer larger than the 64 KB window at `0xA0000` [85]. In 1989 the Video Electronics Standards Association, a hardware consortium, answered with the VESA BIOS Extensions (VBE): a vendor-neutral firmware interface, reached through `INT 10h` function `AX=4Fxx`, that let a program list and set high-resolution modes and find the framebuffer without knowing the specific chip [86][17]. VBE 2.0 (1994) added a linear framebuffer that mapped the whole display in one contiguous block above the 1 MB line, removing the banking penalty, and a later VBE/AF extension tried to standardise basic 2D acceleration [17]. VBE was, in other words, the multi-vendor abstraction a market is supposed to favour — and DirectDraw displaced it completely, on both counts.

Technically, VBE solved the wrong problem. It is a standard for setting modes and finding the framebuffer, not for acceleration: it hands a program a high-resolution linear framebuffer, and the CPU then does all the drawing into it, just as in the DOS software-rendering model of Section 3. It has no notion of a hardware blitter, hardware page-flipping, texture upload, a command stream, or capability negotiation; and VBE/AF's late attempt at 2D acceleration came after the market had moved on, and saw little use. It is also delivered as firmware reached by a software interrupt — the bottleneck Section 5 describes: every VBE call is a BIOS call, and from the protected mode serious software now ran in, each cost a real-mode switch. VBE could expose a framebuffer, but it could not express a graphics processing unit (GPU). As graphics moved from writing the framebuffer to submitting a command buffer consumed by DMA (Section 5), and then to hardware-accelerated 3D (Section 7), VBE had nothing to offer.

Commercially, VBE had no owner with any reason to extend it. It was a consortium standard built as per-card firmware; it gave no one ownership of the platform, so no one had reason to grow it from a framebuffer interface into an acceleration interface. DirectX, by contrast, was owned by Microsoft, tied to Windows, and pushed forward precisely because that ownership was worth something (Sections 1 and 5). VBE also belonged to the model Windows was retiring: a BIOS-interrupt interface is a DOS-era thing, and once the operating system owned the hardware, applications were meant to call the OS driver, not the firmware. So VBE lost on both counts at once — it sat at the wrong layer (firmware, framebuffer-only, interrupt-driven) and had no owner to advance it. The lesson is general: a vendor-neutral standard is necessary but not sufficient. It must also sit where acceleration happens and belong to someone with a reason to develop it. VBE survives today only in the small role it always suited — bringing up a basic linear framebuffer at boot, or as a fallback, before the hardware driver loads.

**WinG (1994).** WinG tackled the gap between DOS and Windows in software alone. It gave the program a `WinGBitmap` that was both a device-independent bitmap and an `HBITMAP` — a direct pointer to the pixels, with a fast transfer path — and ran a one-time test at startup to find the format the installed driver blitted fastest, on the idea that "there will always be a particular WinGBitmap format that is the absolute fastest to blt on the current display" [44][45]. Its aim was to "eliminate the performance difference between DOS and Windows graphics," the standard GDI paths being "three to 20 times slower than BitBlt" [44]. But it set up no new contract for hardware acceleration; it only made *software* blitting as fast as the existing driver allowed, with no route to hardware acceleration and no platform leverage, and DirectX soon replaced it [45].

**DCI (c. 1994).** The Display Control Interface, an Intel–Microsoft standard, was the first contract by which a display driver gave an application direct framebuffer access while staying compatible with GDI: `DCIBeginAccess` "obtains an access pointer to display frame buffer based on the given rectangle," after which the application writes directly until `DCIEndAccess` releases the surface [46][47]. DirectDraw was its successor, turning the locked framebuffer into managed video-memory surfaces with page-flipping and a hardware blitter [31][40].

**RenderMorphics (1995).** Rather than build a 3D API in-house, Microsoft bought one — RenderMorphics, founded in 1992, whose Reality Lab competed with Criterion's RenderWare and Argonaut's BRender — in February 1995 [48][49]; Reality Lab became Direct3D's Retained Mode, layered over a new low-level Immediate Mode [26], buying being the fastest way to the capability.

**OpenGL.** The main alternative Microsoft turned down, and the issue was control of the pipeline. An OpenGL *Installable Client Driver* (ICD) is a vendor DLL, called through `opengl32.dll`, that implements the whole pipeline [50][51]; the *Mini Client Driver* (MCD) was a Microsoft framework in which the vendor accelerated only a subset, with a software fallback [50]; and *miniGL* was a game-specific partial driver from the Quake period (GLQuake, January 1997) [50][52]. The structure is the reverse of Direct3D's DDI: under the DDI, Microsoft's runtime owns the pipeline and the vendor writes callbacks; under an ICD the vendor owns the pipeline. John Carmack argued for OpenGL in December 1996 — "Direct-3D IM is a horribly broken API… there is no good technical reason for the existence of D3D" [53] — and a developer letter repeated the case in 1997. Microsoft declined, casting Direct3D as the consumer games API and OpenGL as the professional one, and kept developing Direct3D [54]. The choice was strategic before it was technical: owning the platform meant owning the API, which a standard Microsoft did not control could not give it.

One feature of OpenGL's design told against DOS in particular, and reinforced the very move the whole strategy was driving toward. The ICD was not portable: it tied an OpenGL rendering context to a GDI device context through the Win32 WGL interface and plugged into the Windows display-driver stack, so it had no home under DOS. Microsoft had shipped OpenGL with Windows NT in 1994 and as an add-on for Windows 95; DOS, by contrast, had only Mesa — an unlicensed, non-conformant software work-alike running under a DOS extender, with no card-vendor drivers and effectively no commercial games [98]. So the very API many developers preferred to Direct3D gave no route at all to *accelerated* 3D on DOS. Together with the Windows-only Direct3D and the vendor-proprietary DOS paths (3dfx's Glide, reached from OpenGL only through a Windows "miniGL" shim), this left every standard road to hardware 3D running through Windows, and pulled 3D development off DOS rather than keeping it there.

OpenGL was the *open, cross-platform* standard — it ran on Unix workstations, and later on Linux and the Macintosh — and so the natural hedge against dependence on Microsoft. Yet on the consumer PC its only accelerated path was the Windows ICD. A developer who chose OpenGL over Direct3D on principle, as Carmack did, still shipped an accelerated game on Windows, because Microsoft controlled the operating system that hosted the only usable PC implementation. The one standard that might have kept 3D gaming off a Microsoft platform reinforced Windows instead.

**Talisman (1996).** Talisman took the ambition from the interface to the hardware itself, aiming for workstation-class 3D at consumer cost by cutting memory bandwidth [55][56]: it rendered in 32 × 32 chunks (cutting frame-buffer memory by about 32× and bandwidth by about 64× [57], with one source citing roughly 60× in image storage [56]); it composited animated objects as separate image layers combined at video refresh, reusing earlier frames through an affine transform [57][58]; and it used a JPEG-like compression scheme, TREC [57]. The "Escalante" reference design split the work across parts from Samsung, Cirrus Logic, and Fujitsu [56][57][58]. No Talisman product reached the market [59]: by the time the design neared production, ordinary accelerators had improved enough that it could not compete, and the partners pulled out around 1997 [59] — a commercial bet that outran what the economics of the silicon could support.

**Fahrenheit (1997–1999).** Fahrenheit took the opposite tack, a cross-vendor unified API built with Silicon Graphics and Hewlett-Packard: SGI's scene graph on top of Microsoft's "Fahrenheit Low Level," "a new low-level rendering engine for Windows… essentially a replacement for Direct3D" [60][61]. It had failed by 1999 for a strategic reason: by SGI's account, Microsoft "had no intention of delivering Low Level," putting little into it while it "invested massively in DirectX 7.0" [61]. A shared standard would have dissolved the very ownership of the API the program was meant to secure.

## 7. The 3D-accelerator market, 1995–2001

The contest over interfaces ran alongside a consolidation of the graphics-hardware market — context that matters, because this consolidation is what made the multi-vendor driver contract economically decisive and removed the one vendor that had rejected it.

**What a 3D accelerator does.** A 3D accelerator puts into fixed-function hardware the pipeline DOS games had run in software: it *rasterises* triangles into pixels, *samples textures* onto them, and sorts out visibility with a *z-buffer* [34][35][36]. Its speed is limited by three rates: the *fill rate* (pixels written per second), the *texel rate* (texture samples per second), and the *memory bandwidth* (how fast the framebuffer and textures can be read and written) [64]. The first accelerators offloaded only rasterisation and texturing; geometry — vertex transform and lighting — stayed on the CPU until it too moved onto the chip. On the PC that geometry ran on the CPU's floating-point unit (FPU), so the processor's floating-point speed set the ceiling on 3D: id Software's Quake (1996) did its geometry entirely in floating point and was hand-tuned by Carmack to the Pentium's pipeline, which left budget chips with weak FPUs — such as Cyrix's 6x86 — barely able to run it, until the GeForce 256 (below) moved transform-and-lighting onto the graphics chip [90].

```
 3dfx Voodoo (1996) — rasteriser only:
   CPU: transform + lighting ──▶ Card: rasterise + texture + z ──▶ VRAM

 GeForce 256 (1999) — adds hardware T&L:
   CPU: submit geometry ──▶ Card: transform + lighting ──▶ rasterise + texture + z ──▶ VRAM

 The geometry stage (transform + lighting) moves off the CPU and onto the card.
```

**The first wave.** Through the early 1990s the board market was a 2D business: S3, ATI, Matrox, Cirrus Logic, Trident, and Tseng Labs made the chips that drew desktops and sped up BitBlt. Consumer 3D arrived as a separate product. 3dfx opened the segment in 1996 with Voodoo Graphics, a 3D-only board with no 2D function, connected by a VGA pass-through cable: the existing 2D card drove the desktop and passed its signal through the Voodoo, which took over the display only during 3D rendering [62][63]. Its API, Glide, ran only on 3dfx hardware [63][72], and had been built because the DirectX of the day "was deemed to be lacking" and OpenGL "was regarded as suitable only for CAD/CAM workstations" [63]. The rivals — Rendition's Vérité, NEC/VideoLogic's tile-based PowerVR, ATI's Rage, Matrox's Mystique, S3's ViRGE (so weak at 3D it was called a "3D decelerator"), and NVIDIA's commercially unsuccessful first chip, the NV1 of 1995 — mostly underperformed [65].

**NVIDIA and hardware transform-and-lighting.** NVIDIA's RIVA 128 (1997), RIVA TNT (1998), and TNT2 (1999) pushed up fill rate and feature coverage, and in October 1999 the GeForce 256 moved geometry onto the card with a hardware transform-and-lighting engine that took vertex work off the CPU [70]. NVIDIA marketed it as "the world's first GPU," defined as a single chip that combined transform, lighting, triangle setup and clipping, and rendering at a minimum of ten million polygons per second [70]. The "first GPU" claim is marketing — 3Dlabs had used the term for a workstation part in 1997 [71] — but the real point stands: the GeForce 256 was the first mainstream consumer card with built-in hardware transform and lighting, until then a workstation feature (S3's Savage 2000 followed within weeks as the only other consumer part to offer it by the end of 1999) [70][71][73].

**The convergence, and the fall of 3dfx.** Glide's advantage depended on 3dfx's dominance, since it ran only on 3dfx hardware; as the market spread across NVIDIA, ATI, Matrox, and S3, a single-vendor API became a liability. Developers wanted one interface that reached every device, and the multi-vendor abstractions gave it to them: Direct3D matured, full OpenGL ICDs shipped from several vendors, and Glide became obsolete — 3dfx releasing it under the GPL in December 1999, after the fact [72]. 3dfx then made a decisive strategic mistake. In December 1998 it bought the board maker STB Systems for about $141 million and integrated vertically, choosing to make and sell cards under its own name rather than license chips to others; the Voodoo3 was the first product of the combined company [66][67][68][69]. The move turned its former customers — Diamond Multimedia and Creative Labs — into competitors, and they shifted their chip orders to NVIDIA [68][69]. Analysts point to this as a main cause of the decline [68]. Without board partners and late with its next generation, 3dfx failed; NVIDIA bought its assets, mostly the intellectual property, on 15 December 2000 [63]. The outcome confirms the platform argument: the most direct and fastest design of its day — Glide on the Voodoo — won on the benchmarks but lost on the platform, because a single-vendor API cannot last in a multi-vendor market. Glide also shows that the constraint was never the hardware: the same Voodoo silicon was hardware-accelerated under *both* DOS — through the `glide2x.ovl` driver, in titles such as the 3dfx builds of Tomb Raider and Descent II — and Windows, through `glide2x.dll` [99]. So what DOS lacked was not a path to the accelerator, which a single-vendor API supplied on both systems, but the *multi-vendor* driver contract above it, and a platform owner who could force one into being. The market rewarded that OS-mediated, multi-vendor contract over the fastest single device: two vendors were left, NVIDIA and ATI, and it was NVIDIA — having absorbed its last big rival — that Microsoft picked to supply the graphics chip for its console.

## 8. 2001: the Xbox and Windows XP

By the late 1990s DirectX had met its first goal: Windows had become the platform for PC game development. The competitive threat then shifted to the console, and Microsoft answered by extending the platform strategy into that market. In 2001 the two phases came together: Microsoft entered the console market with the Xbox and retired the DOS-based consumer line with Windows XP.

The immediate threat was Sony's PlayStation 2, announced on 2 March 1999 [74]. The worry was strategic, not about any one product: the PS2 was pitched as a broad entertainment box — with DVD and CD playback — that was drawing in PC game developers and might make the living room, rather than the Windows PC, the centre of home computing. The Xbox project was "intended to counter the perceived threat to the Windows franchise posed by Sony expanding the PlayStation into a broad entertainment platform" [75][74], and it started inside the graphics group: within a month of the PS2 announcement, four members of Microsoft's DirectX and graphics group — Otto Berkes (the DirectX and OpenGL development lead), Seamus Blackley, Kevin Bachus, and Ted Hase — proposed a Microsoft console built on DirectX to counter Sony [74][76]. The name records the lineage: it is short for "DirectX Box," with the working "X-box" meant as a code name until an agency settled on "Xbox." In Blackley's words, "A box for DirectX. So it was the DirectX box. And eventually, we called it Xbox" [75][77].

The common claim that the Xbox sprang from Bill Gates's personal interest in gaming is unsupported, and the idea that he set out that interest in his 1995 book *The Road Ahead* is wrong: the book is about the personal-computing revolution and the "information superhighway," and says nothing about video games, consoles, or the games market [83]. Gates was not the project's champion but a sceptic the team had to win over, at a review on 5 May 1999 where Blackley argued that porting from PC to console would be easy "because they shared a common DirectX heritage," beating a rival proposal based on Windows CE that was incompatible with the current DirectX [74][76]. The argument that the console would help Windows won his approval; by insider accounts he later objected when the project moved to a closed, custom operating system and the expected code did not come back to the Windows group [76][78][82]. What drove the project was a defensive read of the Sony threat, not an enthusiasm for gaming.

The technical continuity was real, and it pulls together every mechanism this essay has described. The Xbox shipped in November 2001 with a custom 733 MHz Intel Pentium III — an x86 PC processor; an NVIDIA NV2A graphics chip based on the GeForce 3, from the vendor that had just consolidated the accelerator market; an operating system based on the Windows NT/2000 kernel; and a graphics API based on Direct3D [79]. The console was, in effect, a fixed-configuration DirectX PC: the same instruction set, the same graphics vendor and pipeline, the same API model, and the same kernel lineage the PC platform had produced. That lineage reached the consumer desktop the same year, when Windows XP (2001) moved the consumer line off the DOS-based 9x foundation and onto the protected NT/2000 kernel [84], ending the DOS-based consumer line — the last step of the move DirectX had begun by drawing developers away from DOS.

From nothing, Microsoft made itself a permanent power in a market it had never contested, and against the most successful console ever made. The original Xbox sold about 24 million units to the PlayStation 2's 155 to 160 million [80][81]; the Xbox 360 (2005) reached about 84 million, near parity with its generation's leader [80]; the Xbox One later lost to the PlayStation 4 by about two to one [80]. Across three generations Microsoft held a durable top-two position behind Sony without ever taking the lead. The same playbook that won the PC carried Microsoft into the console business and made it formidable there, but owning the technical platform was not, on its own, enough to unseat an entrenched incumbent. Platform control proved necessary to compete and endure, and insufficient to dominate outright.

## 9. Conclusion

This history is the story of one boundary — control of the hardware — moving under the joint pressure of commercial strategy and technical constraint. Under DOS there was no boundary, because real mode could not enforce one: the application controlled the hardware, abstraction came by convention on interrupt vectors, and what passed for "performance" was just the absence of any layer in the way of a small framebuffer; commercially, the platform was un-owned, and its directness kept developers on it. Windows handed control of the hardware to the operating system, behind memory protection and a driver model, and in doing so created both the commercial asset and the technical obstacle; DirectX cleared the obstacle and took the asset, giving a fast, controlled path — dedicated hardware fed by direct memory access, over a widening bus, through driver-built command buffers — that beat DOS performance and made Windows the platform for game development, on Microsoft's own interface. DirectX won out over its alternatives because its placement of the boundary — a Microsoft-controlled runtime above vendor-written callbacks — was the one arrangement in which the commercial and technical aims lined up; where they did not, the alternative failed: Talisman outran what the silicon economics could bear, Fahrenheit and Glide both clashed with API ownership, and Glide showed as well that a single-vendor interface cannot last in a multi-vendor market. The hardware shows this directly: the same chip a DOS game drove directly, a Windows driver drove through the operating system, so the constraint was never physical — what DOS lacked was a platform owner who could spread driver work across the hardware industry and supply the operating-system services a driver needs. When a console threatened that ownership, Microsoft extended the same strategy and the same DirectX stack — x86 CPU, NVIDIA GPU, NT kernel — into the Xbox, and in the same year retired DOS on the desktop with Windows XP. The console bet paid off on its own terms: Microsoft entered a market it had never contested and made itself a permanent power there, second only to Sony. The one place the playbook met its limit is that it never overtook Sony — owning the technical platform was enough to compete and endure, not to dominate outright. The arc of the history is the transfer of hardware control from the application to the operating system, and the long commercial and technical effort to hold the position that transfer created.

## A note on method and evidence

The essay combines a commercial analysis with a technical one, and the evidence base is correspondingly mixed. The Windows graphics driver mechanisms (Sections 4–5) are sourced to primary vendor and standards documentation — Microsoft's driver documentation for the DDI, HAL, DCI, and ICD/MCD models, Chris Hecker's WinG article, and the opengl.org FAQ — and the core of that material was confirmed through multi-vote adversarial verification. The scalability mechanism of the driver contract (Section 5) — one contract defined by Microsoft, one driver per device written by each hardware vendor — together with the demonstration that identical accelerator silicon was reachable from both DOS and Windows (the Glide/Voodoo case, Section 7) and that accelerated OpenGL on the consumer PC ran only through the Windows ICD (Section 6), was verified the same way in a later round; two qualifications were deliberately preserved in the text — that an operating system also supplies driver services DOS lacked (memory management, Plug-and-Play, AGP remapping), and that Microsoft actively co-designed and co-implemented the stack rather than merely publishing an interface. The hardware and operating-system internals (Sections 2–7) — processor modes, the memory map, I/O and interrupts and DMA, VGA organisation, the SVGA/VESA VBE standard, the graphics pipeline — are standard, textbook-level facts cited to canonical references; they were written from established knowledge and cited, not re-run through the adversarial workflow. The accelerator history (Section 7) is sourced to primary and reputable secondary material, with dates fixed (the $141 million STB acquisition of December 1998, NVIDIA's acquisition of 3dfx's assets on 15 December 2000, the GeForce 256 of October 1999) and two overstatements corrected: NVIDIA's "world's first GPU" is marketing, as 3Dlabs used the term in 1997, though the GeForce 256 was the first mainstream consumer device with hardware transform and lighting; and the claim that mismanaged Rampage delays alone caused 3dfx's failure did not survive verification, the STB vertical-integration decision being the better-documented cause. The strategic and Xbox material (Sections 1, 5, 8) rests in part on interested first-person testimony — St. John, Berkes, Blackley, Nat Brown, via Dean Takahashi's reporting — reliable for stated intent but not neutral on motive, and identified as testimony; the "DirectX Box" naming, the four-member DirectX-team origin, the PS2-threat motive, the CPU/GPU continuity, and the sales figures were adversarially verified, and three overstated versions were refuted and excluded. Two premises were checked and corrected: that the Xbox originated in Bill Gates' personal interest in gaming (he was a skeptic who required persuasion; the driver was a defensive assessment of the Sony threat), and that he set out a gaming objective in *The Road Ahead* (the 1995 book addresses the information superhighway and does not discuss games — verified). The account of why the PC became the dominant gaming platform (Section 1) is drawn from standard encyclopedic and reputable secondary sources, and gives the open-architecture and competitor-collapse factors primacy over raw CPU/FPU performance, which was decisive specifically in the 3D era. John Romero's "DOS Blows" remark (Section 3) is attributed to David Kushner's *Masters of Doom* on the strength of a reader's recollection; its exact wording could not be independently verified online, and it is presented as a reported quotation. Console sales figures are stated at the conservative end of published ranges. Quantitative figures throughout — transition costs, bus bandwidths, compression ratios, unit sales — are given at the level of magnitude.
